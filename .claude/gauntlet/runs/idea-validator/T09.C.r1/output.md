## The number that matters most

Work this through with your real figures, but here's the shape of it. Per weekly box (5 dinners for two = 10 portions, R1 190):

| Line | Metro delivery | Outlying / nationwide |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | R1 190 | R1 190 |
| Food COGS (~R40/portion at low volume) | −R400 | −R400 |
| Insulated packaging, gel packs, box, cards | −R90 | −R90 |
| Cold-chain courier | −R160 | −R350 |
| Payment gateway (~3.5%) | −R42 | −R42 |
| Spoilage/wastage (~8% of food) | −R32 | −R32 |
| **Contribution per box** | **~R466 (39%)** | **~R276 (23%)** |

Now the fixed costs. A small commercial kitchen, a chef, one prep hand, a cold room, compliance and insurance realistically lands somewhere around R50 000–R70 000 a month. Call it R60 000.

**R60 000 ÷ R466 = 129 boxes a month = about 32 active weekly subscribers to break even.** Before marketing. Before you pay yourself anything.

You have a 60-person free waitlist. At a normal waitlist-to-paid conversion of 5–15%, that's **3 to 9 subscribers**. You would need every single person on that list to convert, twice over, just to reach breakeven.

If you strip fixed costs hard — hire kitchen time by the hour instead of renting, no salary for yourself, no prep assistant until volume justifies one — you might get fixed down to R25 000–R30 000 and breakeven to 15–16 subscribers. That's still a quarter of your entire waitlist converting at 100%. Tight, but it's at least in the same postcode as reality. **The gap between those two setups is the difference between having twelve months of runway and having three.** If you do one structural thing before the 1st, make it that one.

## Where I think the strategic flaw actually is

The value proposition is medical — carb-counted, dietitian-signed. The delivery format is a **meal kit**, which means the customer still shops nothing but still cooks, still portions, still has to execute the recipe correctly for the carb count to mean anything.

A diabetic's actual jobs-to-be-done are roughly: keep post-meal glucose in range, stop having to think about it, don't feel deprived, and sustain it affordably *forever*. A fresh kit serves the second one partially and fails the fourth badly at R119 a portion. That's R5 150 a month for dinner only — one meal a day, for two people, five days a week. The rest of their eating is unsolved.

That's the vulnerability. You're charging premium-convenience prices for a product that still asks the customer to cook, in a category where the entrenched alternative is *subsidised by their medical scheme*.

## What the 14 months actually bought you

This matters, so I want to be precise about it: **the money is not wasted, and the asset you built is probably not the one you think it is.**

What you own now is (a) a dietitian-validated, carb-counted recipe library, (b) a clean brand in a category where nobody in SA has planted a flag on diabetes specifically, and (c) direct access to a customer with a chronic, recurring, medically-motivated need — which is the highest-lifetime-value customer profile in food, *if* you can reach them at a price they can sustain.

None of that is locked to "fresh kits, nationwide." Three directions preserve all of it:

**1. Frozen heat-and-eat instead of fresh kits.** Removes cooking friction entirely (better on the actual JTBD), tolerates a 24–48 hour courier window instead of demanding an unbroken chilled chain, slashes spoilage, and lets you batch-produce. It also makes nationwide *actually* viable later, which chilled never will be.

**2. Sell to the payer, not the patient.** This is the big one. Diabetes is extraordinarily expensive for SA medical schemes — the complications far more than the condition. If you can produce even modest evidence that your meals move HbA1c, you convert a consumer-affordability problem into a reimbursement conversation with a scheme or a managed-care provider. That is precisely why the dietitian sign-off has commercial value rather than just marketing value. It's slow, it's relationship-driven, and it's the only version of this where the price stops being the obstacle. Employer wellness programmes at large SA corporates are the same logic with a shorter sales cycle.

**3. Own the clinical referral channel.** The moment of maximum willingness to change behaviour is *diagnosis*. Diabetes nurse educators, endocrinologists, GPs and pharmacy clinics are your distribution, not Facebook. One good relationship with a diabetes educator is worth more than the waitlist.

Worth naming honestly: option 2 is the one with the biggest prize and it does not fit a fourteen-month-savings-runway timeline. If you go that way, go with your eyes open about needing income from somewhere else while it develops.

## Launch on the 1st — but instrument it

Do launch. The packaging is bought, the waitlist is warm, and you cannot buy this information any cheaper than by selling to real people. Three rules:

**Charge full price. Do not discount the first box.** A discount destroys the only signal you're actually launching to collect — willingness to pay — and it reliably recruits the cohort that churns. You need the truth more than you need the first ten orders.

**Measure these five things, weekly, per delivery zone — not blended:**
1. Waitlist → paid conversion (below 20% means the offer or the price is wrong)
2. Week-4 repeat rate (above 60% is healthy; below 40% is a kill signal)
3. Fully-loaded contribution per box, split metro vs. outlying
4. Cost to acquire one customer who was *not* on the waitlist — the waitlist is a one-time asset, this is the real number
5. Percentage of orders going outside your home metro (above 20% and the logistics are bleeding you)

**Pre-commit to a stop rule now, in writing, while you're calm.** Something like: *if by end of week 6 I have fewer than 15 paying weekly subscribers and week-4 repeat is under 50%, I stop restocking and move to the frozen or B2B version.* Decide it now, because you will not be able to decide it fairly in week six.
